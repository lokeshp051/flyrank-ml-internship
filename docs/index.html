FlyRank ML Internship — Capstone Research Paper

Which content pages lose search visibility first — and can a model queue them better than a hand-written rule?

By Lokesh · github.com/lokeshp051/flyrank-ml-internship

Abstract

A content reviewer can only check so many pages a week, so this paper asks a narrow question: given a page's first-half-of-month search signals, can a model rank which pages are declining better than a simple hand-written rule?

Using March 2026 daily search performance data from FlyRank's internship warehouse, joined against static content attributes, I built a transparent baseline (flag top-ranking pages with below-median click-through for their position) and compared it against a depth-limited Decision Tree trained on the same five knowable-in-advance features. On a client-grouped 80/20 split — so no client's pages appear in both train and test — the baseline wins narrowly at the very top of the queue (Precision@20: 0.55 vs 0.50), but the model pulls ahead once a reviewer can check deeper into the list (Precision@50: 0.64 vs 0.56; Precision@100: 0.68 vs 0.54). The result is a three-tier reason-coded queue meant to sit in front of a human reviewer, not replace one.

1 — Introduction

The problem: limited eyes, too many pages

A content team can review a handful of pages a week — not the whole site. The question isn't "is this page declining," which is easy to answer in hindsight; it's "which pages should a reviewer open first," which has to be answered before the decline is obvious to a human eye.

The decision this supports is concrete: a content reviewer starts each week with a ranked list and works down it until their time runs out. Getting the order right matters more than getting every page right, because the pages near the bottom of the list may never get looked at.

2 — Data

Source, window, and exclusions

Data comes from the FlyRank Internship Warehouse Release (v20260703), table fact_content_daily_performance (daily grain, partitioned by month), joined with dim_content for static page attributes.

The analysis window is March 2026 (month=2026-03), a mid-panel month. June 2026, the final month in the release, is treated as a sealed test set and was deliberately not touched during this work.

Two things were excluded on purpose: fact_content_query_90d (query-level data, out of scope for a page-level decision) and the AI-referral columns, since a causal relationship between AI-referral volume and decline wasn't established going in.

All content and client identifiers in the warehouse are pseudonymized hashes. No raw client names, domains, or search queries appear anywhere in this analysis, the underlying notebooks, or this page.

3 — Methodology

Label, features, baseline, model, validation

Label (proxy). is_declining_label is set when a page's impressions in the second half of March are lower than in the first half. It's a within-month proxy, not a verified business outcome — flagged again in Limitations below.

Features. Five signals, all knowable before the decision point since they're drawn only from the first half of the month: impressions_h1, clicks_h1, avg_position_h1, ctr_h1, and word_count.

Baseline. A hand-written rule: flag any page ranking top-20 or better whose click-through rate sits below the median for its position bucket (LOW_CTR_FOR_POSITION). No model, no training — just a threshold a reviewer could compute in a spreadsheet.

Model. A Decision Tree Classifier (max_depth=4, class_weight="balanced") — kept shallow so it stays as readable as the baseline while combining more than one signal.

Validation. A GroupShuffleSplit (80/20, grouped by client) — no client's pages appear in both the training and test sets, which simulates deploying the model against a client it has never seen.

Leakage check. Confirmed no feature is a near-perfect stand-in for the label-generating column (second-half impressions). The highest correlation was impressions_h1 at 0.83 — expected autocorrelation between a page's traffic in the two halves of a month, not leakage.

The rule wins at the very top of the queue; the model pulls ahead once a reviewer can work deeper into it — a pattern that also showed up in an earlier toy example during Week 2 of the internship.

The model's split is dominated by one feature: word_count carries roughly 0.42 of total importance — more than impressions_h1 (0.20), ctr_h1 (0.18), avg_position_h1 (0.15), and clicks_h1 (0.06) combined. That's addressed directly in Limitations, since it wasn't a signal the baseline rule considered at all.

5 — Limitations & honest framing

What this work cannot claim

  • This is an observational, single-month analysis. Correlations here are not causal — nothing in this paper implies that changing a feature would change the outcome.
  • The label is a proxy (impressions dropped within the same month), not a verified downstream business outcome like lost revenue or churned traffic.
  • word_count's outsized importance (0.42) is unexpected and may reflect a correlated content-type effect — page template, content category — rather than a direct causal lever a writer can pull.
  • GA4 coverage in this slice is sparse (roughly 4% of rows), which limited how much cross-source (behavioral) features could be brought in.
  • Results are specific to this portfolio slice and this month. They should not be read as universal SEO claims, and haven't been tested against the sealed June 2026 window.
6 — Ranked recommendations

What a reviewer does with this

The model plus a reason-code layer sorts flagged pages into three tiers. All three require a human to look before anything happens — nothing here is meant to auto-publish or auto-delete a page.

HIGH_RISK_LOW_CTR

Urgent refresh and snippet rewrite — declining pages with a click-through problem specifically, where the title/snippet is the likely lever.

HIGH_RISK_GENERAL

Scheduled content refresh — declining pages without a clean single cause; needs an editorial look, not just a snippet edit.

LOW_CTR_FOR_POSITION

Title/snippet review — pages that aren't yet flagged as declining but are underperforming their position, worth a lower-urgency look.

Monitoring. Track real impression change 30 and 60 days after any action, against a matched control group of similar pages that weren't touched. Retrain the model if Precision@50 drops meaningfully below 0.64 on fresh data, or on a quarterly cadence regardless.
